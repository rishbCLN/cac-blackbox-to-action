\begin{abstract}
A counterfactual state that changes a classifier's decision need not describe an
action whose consequences preserve that decision. We study this distinction
using Causal Actionable Counterfactuals, an implementation that searches over
interventions and evaluates the states produced by a structural causal model.
The evaluation separates proposed-state validity, replayed-action success,
action feasibility, and state fidelity. A plan is usable under the specified
model only when the same suggestion passes replay and feasibility. We audit
saved experiments on Adult, German Credit, HELOC, and diabetes, comprising
\TotalApplicants{} rejected applicants in the primary comparison. At the
canonical search seed, the intervention method provides usable recourse for
every queried applicant, while several baselines tie it on three datasets.
On HELOC, usable rates range from \HelocWachterUsable\% to
\HelocCacUsable\%; proposed-state validity alone obscures much of this gap.
However, saved search repetitions expose incomplete seed coverage and generation
failures: the intervention method's mean usable rate on diabetes is
\DiabetesCacSeedMean\%. Recomputed applicant-level inference avoids treating
repeated seeds as independent people. A refitted-model sensitivity analysis and
two synthetic worlds further delimit the shared-model findings. These results
support reporting replay success and feasibility alongside predictive validity,
while providing no guarantee of real-world intervention effects or universal
method superiority.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{A}{lgorithmic} recourse asks what a person can change after receiving an
unfavorable prediction. A common computational answer is a nearby feature
vector that receives the desired label. This answer has a precise predictive
meaning: the classifier accepts that vector. Its interpretation as advice
requires additional assumptions about which coordinates a person controls and
how the rest of the record responds.

For example, an applicant may change education while occupation and hours
subsequently respond. Directly editing all three coordinates describes a
different action from intervening on education alone. A method that reports a
resulting state therefore needs to distinguish its requested interventions from
their predicted consequences. The distinction between feature changes and
interventions is established in causal recourse research~\cite{karimi2021}.
Our work uses that distinction to organize an empirical audit of an existing
implementation and its saved experiments.

The implementation, Causal Actionable Counterfactuals (\CAC), uses a population
search over a bounded intervention set. Each candidate passes through a fitted
structural causal model (SCM) before its classifier score is evaluated. The
resulting search space is internally consistent with that model, subject to
the implementation's encoding and rounding. Internal consistency alone does
not establish that a favorable outcome is reachable, that the search will find
it, or that the assumed causal equations describe the world.

This paper asks three questions. First, how much does an evaluation based only
on proposed-state validity conceal about replay success and action constraints?
Second, what changes when causal propagation and objective terms are removed
from the same search? Third, how do search repetitions, alternative fitted
equations, and known synthetic mechanisms limit the primary result?

The contribution is an evidence-based evaluation framework and a verified
account of this implementation. We define all endpoints at the suggestion and
applicant levels, reconstruct the primary table from proposal-level artifacts,
and replay the saved plans against the stored models. We also recompute paired
inference using applicants as independent units, retaining incomplete runs and
recorded failures. The findings establish useful computational distinctions;
they do not introduce intervention-based recourse as a new concept.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Counterfactual explanations and feasible changes}
Wachter \emph{et al.}~\cite{wachter} formulate counterfactual explanations
through alternative inputs that receive a desired decision. DiCE promotes
diverse alternatives so that a person has multiple routes to consider
\cite{dice}. Ustun \emph{et al.}~\cite{ustun} explicitly constrain allowable
feature changes for linear classifiers. These perspectives motivate separating
the existence of a favorable feature vector from the feasibility of acting
on it. The present comparisons use the repository's concrete adaptations of
these ideas; they are not evaluations of every published variant.

\subsection{Causal recourse and model uncertainty}
Karimi \emph{et al.}~\cite{karimi2021} establish intervention-based recourse:
an action should be evaluated through a causal model rather than by assuming
that all unchanged coordinates remain fixed. Their work is the closest
conceptual foundation of \CAC{}. Recourse under imperfect causal knowledge
further addresses uncertainty about those mechanisms~\cite{karimi2020}.
Our fitted-model rescore and synthetic audit are sensitivity analyses; they
do not supply a posterior distribution over possible causal worlds.

Abduction, intervention, and prediction follow the structural causal
perspective of Pearl~\cite{pearl}. A hand-specified graph combined with flexible
regressors remains an assumed causal model. Conditional association tests can
challenge some implications of that model but cannot identify all causal
directions or individual effects. We consequently use the expression
``usable under the specified SCM and constraints'' throughout.

\subsection{Scope of the empirical comparison}
The primary saved experiment contains Random, a Wachter-style optimization,
DiCE-random, a finite-grid point reference, and \CAC{}. A minimal-cost causal
intervention implementation, MINT, exists in the code and appears in the
synthetic experiments. It is absent from the saved observational headline
and search-seed comparison. We therefore make no observational claim that
\CAC{} improves on MINT or on the strongest available causal optimizer.

\section{Problem Formulation and Evaluation Endpoints}
\label{sec:problem}
Let $x\in\mathcal{X}$ be an encoded feature vector, $f$ a fixed binary
classifier, and $p_{+}(x)$ its favorable-class probability. The favorable
decision is $f(x)=1$ at threshold $\tau=\DecisionThreshold$. We explain only
test records for which $f(x)=0$. An explanation comprises a proposed state
$z$ and a declared action $a=(S,v_S)$, where $S$ is a feature subset and
$v_S$ gives its requested values. The classifier receives features only;
an outcome node in an SCM graph is not an input coordinate.

\subsection{Replay of the declared action}
For a specified SCM, the action is replayed as
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde{x}=\Replay(x,a)
   =\SCM_{\mathrm{do}(X_S=v_S)}(u_x),
  \label{eq:replay}
\end{equation}
where $u_x$ denotes the factual exogenous state inferred by abduction. The
implementation propagates descendants in topological order and applies the
feature encodings, bounds, and discrete rounding used by its saved models.

The audit uses the explicitly declared feature set, stored as
\artifact{changed_features}. It does not infer extra interventions from every
coordinate that differs between $x$ and $z$. This matters for methods such as
\CAC{}, which report consequences as well as requested actions. If $S$ is
empty, replay returns the factual record. An unexplained favorable proposal
therefore receives no credit simply because its state differs from $x$.

\subsection{Four measurements and one primary endpoint}
For each suggestion, proposed-state validity and replay success are
\begin{align}
 V(x,z)&=\ind\{p_{+}(z)\geq\tau\},\label{eq:valid}\\
 R(x,a)&=\ind\{p_{+}(\widetilde{x})\geq\tau\}.\label{eq:replayed}
\end{align}
Actionability, $A(x,a)$, requires admissible features and directions,
at most \MaxActions{} effective interventions, and normalized effort at most
one. Plausibility is reported separately and is not an actionability gate.

To measure agreement between the promised state and the replayed state,
let $s_j$ be feature $j$'s encoded range, lower bounded away from zero:
\begin{align}
 \Delta(x,z,a)&=\max_j\frac{|z_j-\widetilde{x}_j|}{s_j},\label{eq:gap}\\
 F(x,z,a)&=V(x,z)\,\ind\{\Delta(x,z,a)\leq\epsilon\},
 \quad \epsilon=\FidelityTolerance.\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{align}
This is a range-normalized tolerance, not a standard-deviation tolerance.
For categorical coordinates, the numerical gap is an encoding-dependent
diagnostic and should not be interpreted as an intrinsic semantic distance.

The primary endpoint is
\begin{equation}
  U(x,z,a)=R(x,a)A(x,a).\label{eq:usable}
\end{equation}
Neither $V$ nor $F$ gates $U$. A suggestion may fail at its named state but
succeed after its consequences are propagated; a favorable replay can also
remain usable while poorly describing its resulting state.

\subsection{Aggregation and denominators}
For applicant $i$ and method $m$, with up to $k=\CounterfactualCount$
suggestions, define
\begin{equation}
 U_{im}=\max_{\ell\leq k}\bigl(R_{im\ell}A_{im\ell}\bigr),
 \qquad \widehat{U}_{m}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{im}.
 \label{eq:applicant}
\end{equation}
The same suggestion must satisfy both conditions. Taking the maximum of
replay success and the maximum of feasibility separately could credit
different plans and would produce an incorrect endpoint. A method returning
no suggestion contributes a failure for that applicant; the denominator
remains the full query set. The other applicant-level columns use their
own per-suggestion predicate, so they need not form a nested funnel.

\section{Implementation of Causal Actionable Counterfactuals}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Structural equations}
The repository specifies a directed acyclic graph for each dataset. Continuous
nodes use fitted conditional means with additive factual residuals; discrete
nodes use fitted conditional class probabilities with a fixed factual noise
coupling. Histogram gradient boosting supplies the conditional estimators.
Only descendants of intervened nodes are recomputed, and factual noise is
reused across candidate actions for the same applicant. These choices provide
a reproducible counterfactual coupling, not an identified individual mechanism.

\subsection{Action effort and feasibility}
Each feature has an effort function appropriate to its declared representation:
linear or piecewise effort for numeric changes, ladder costs for ordinal
levels, and entry costs for categorical switches. The action effort is
\begin{equation}
  C(x,a)=\frac{\sum_{j\in S} w_jc_j(x_j,v_j)}{B},
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where the dataset-specific $B$ sums the largest weighted maximum costs among
the allowed number of actionable features. This normalizer reflects the
configured action space. It does not measure an applicant's actual resources.

Costs and feasibility apply to requested interventions. A descendant that
changes through propagation is not charged as an additional action. The audit
nevertheless records side effects, including sensitive-feature changes.
Hard restrictions on non-manipulable features and monotone directions are
applied independently of the soft feasibility objective.

\subsection{Search objective}
Let $q=\tau+\TargetMargin$ for the favorable class, capped at one. Candidate
actions are scored by
\begin{align}
 J(a;x)={}&\lambda_v\frac{[q-p_{+}(\Replay(x,a))]_+}{q}
       +\lambda_c C(x,a)\nonumber\\
       &+\lambda_f H(x,a)+\lambda_p P(\Replay(x,a)),
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
with weights $(\lambda_v,\lambda_c,\lambda_f,\lambda_p)=
(\WeightValid,\WeightCost,\WeightFeas,\WeightPlaus)$.
$H$ combines the normalized inadmissibility penalty and sensitive-feature
side effects. The hard legalization step typically makes the former zero.
$P$ is a range-normalized $\KnnK$-nearest-neighbor distance to favorable-class
training records, divided by a typical within-class reference distance.
This plausibility penalty influences selection but is not a proof of
distributional support.

\subsection{Population search and reporting}
Candidate values come from feature-specific grids built from training values
or quantiles, with \GridPoints{} quantile locations for numeric grids. A
population of \PopulationSize{} candidates mixes singleton interventions with
random multi-feature actions. Each generation retains the best
\ElitePercent\%, applies uniform crossover, jitters selected values, and
mutates the action subset. Legalization snaps values back to admissible grids
and caps the intervention count. Search ends after at most
\MaxGenerations{} generations or \SearchPatience{} generations without a
sufficient objective improvement.

The archive retains classifier-accepted candidates for later selection.
If insufficient accepted candidates exist, the method can return best
available non-flipping proposals; the external audit still scores them as
failures. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cac} summarizes the search. It gives no global
optimality or completeness guarantee. Batched classifier calls reduce
overhead, while query counts charge scored candidate rows rather than
treating an entire batch as one evaluation.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Intervention-space search used by \CAC}
\label{alg:cac}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Rejected $x$, classifier $p_+$, SCM $\SCM$, legal grids, $k$
\State Abduct factual noise $u_x$; initialize legal action population
\State Initialize archive of favorable candidate states
\For{each generation until iteration or patience limit}
  \For{each candidate action $a=(S,v_S)$}
    \State Replay $a$ using $u_x$ to obtain $\widetilde{x}$
    \State Score $p_+(\widetilde{x})$ and objective~\eqref{eq:objective}
    \State Archive favorable states with their declared actions
  \EndFor
  \State Retain elites; crossover, mutate, and legalize children
\EndFor
\State Select up to $k$ distinct proposals using the stored objectives
\State \Return states, declared actions, effort and query metadata
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The query selector takes the first at most configured number of rejected test
records in index order. It does not sample applicants afresh for each method.
Each method receives the same records and returns up to
\CounterfactualCount{} suggestions. The canonical search seed is
\artifact{42}. Search-seed repetitions keep the predictive model, training
split, cost model, and SCM fixed.

\subsection{Baseline implementations and access assumptions}
Random samples candidate feature changes. The Wachter-style implementation
uses finite-difference L-BFGS-B with restarts, and DiCE uses the library's
random method. The finite-grid reference performs cost-ordered direct point
search with an evaluation cap. It is called ``oracle'' in stored artifacts,
but its feasible set differs from causal intervention search. Its cost is
therefore neither a universal causal lower bound nor an optimality certificate
for \CAC{}.

All primary methods operate through prediction interfaces. The baselines
receive immutability restrictions, but their optimization procedures and
query budgets are different. Finite differences can be a poor fit to a
piecewise-constant tree ensemble. Query counts are consequently reported as
descriptive computational costs, not as evidence from an equal-budget contest.
The strongest causal baseline available in the source, MINT, optimizes action
cost through the SCM but has only synthetic results in the saved comparisons
used here.

\subsection{Verification against saved artifacts}
The new evidence builder reconstructs the main table from
\artifact{counterfactuals.csv}, \artifact{faithfulness_per_cf.csv}, and
\artifact{feasibility_per_cf.csv}. It verifies unique suggestion keys,
matching applicant sets, audit coverage, and agreement with all reported
headline fields. It additionally reloads each dataset's saved counterfactual
objects and recomputes their metrics, declared-action replay, feasibility,
and headline summaries against the currently stored models.

Saved cost ratios differ from ratios recomputed under the current default
filtering convention. Rather than mixing these conventions, this paper
reports absolute normalized action effort. The discrepancy is retained in the
verification outputs. Predictive metrics are cross-checked against
\artifact{models/metrics.json}; the report includes SHA-256 hashes of the
inputs and relevant code. This verifies the saved evidence and current replay;
it does not claim that every expensive search experiment was rerun.

A fresh local execution of the project's test suite produced
\TestsPassed{} passes, \TestsSkipped{} skip, and no failures or setup errors.
The skipped test concerns dropping a sub-plan when the generated action has
only one feature. Unit tests establish software properties on their fixtures;
they do not validate a causal graph or an empirical superiority claim.

\subsection{Applicant-level statistical analysis}
Each baseline is paired with \CAC{} on the identical applicant at the
canonical seed. We use the exact two-sided McNemar test and apply Holm
correction at familywise level 0.05 within each dataset. The paired sample
size is the number of distinct applicants, never applicants multiplied by
search seeds.

For effect sizes, we first average each applicant's paired usability
difference over shared available seeds, then bootstrap whole applicants with
replacement using 10,000 resamples at seed 42. The interval
therefore describes variation across queried applicants conditional on the
saved split and models. Its estimand differs from the single-seed binary test.
Observed per-seed means and ranges are reported separately, with the actual
number of available seed runs. Missing runs are not imputed and recorded
generation exceptions remain failures.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Primary comparison: replay and feasibility expose different failures}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} reports the reconstructed canonical-seed comparison.
\CAC{} attains usable recourse for all \TotalApplicants{} queried applicants
under the shared SCM and constraints. This outcome does not imply uniform
superiority: Random and DiCE-random tie it on Adult and German, and several
methods tie it on diabetes. The minimum saved usable rate in this table is
\HelocWachterUsable\%, not the lower percentage stated in an older manuscript.

HELOC exhibits the clearest separation. Random has
\HelocRandomValidity\% proposed-state validity but \HelocRandomUsable\%
usable recourse; DiCE-random has \HelocDiceValidity\% and
\HelocDiceUsable\%, respectively. The Wachter-style method has
\HelocWachterValidity\% proposed-state validity,
\HelocWachterCausalvalidity\% replay success, and
\HelocWachterActionability\% actionability, with only
\HelocWachterUsable\% of applicants receiving a plan that passes both latter
tests. These rates cannot be multiplied to recover usability because they
are applicant-level marginal events over multiple suggestions.

\subsection{Effort and computational cost do not support a universal ranking}
\CAC{}'s mean normalized effort is \AdultCacCost{} on Adult,
\GermanCacCost{} on German, \HelocCacCost{} on HELOC, and
\DiabetesCacCost{} on diabetes. The Adult Random baseline achieves the
same applicant coverage at lower reported effort. In contrast, \CAC{} on
HELOC has higher usable coverage than Random and lower conditional effort.
These comparisons describe the saved outputs: effort averages condition
on each method's own successful applicants and do not constitute a paired
common-success cost comparison.

The recorded query counts also vary substantially across methods and datasets.
A low count can accompany a less reliable proposal, and an expensive search can
still fail the action audit. Neither query count nor the objective value alone
summarizes the coverage--effort tradeoff. A future equal-budget study should
compare success curves and paired costs on a prespecified common-success set.

\section{Limitations and Responsible Interpretation}
\label{sec:limitations}
\subsection{Predictive protocol and data provenance}
The saved predictive metrics are internally consistent with their model
reports, but the training protocol is not a fully isolated test evaluation.
The model-training code checks test AUC to decide whether to trigger a
hyperparameter search. Although the final tuned/default choice uses training
cross-validation, the decision to search has already consulted the test set.
In addition, HELOC and diabetes median imputation occurs before the split,
and the diabetes cleanup uses class-dependent patterns to identify a
redistributed source's suspected label-dependent fill values. These steps
limit claims about out-of-sample predictive performance.

A stronger confirmatory experiment should split before estimating any
preprocessing quantities, retain an original missingness mask and verified
source, and make all tuning decisions using training/validation data.
The current paper preserves the measured results and discloses their
protocol rather than asserting that an artifact consistency check repairs
these design limitations.

\subsection{Causal uncertainty and operational meaning}
The learned equations, discrete noise coupling, graph directions, cost
levels, and legal interventions are assumptions. Passing their audit means
that a computational plan is consistent with those assumptions. It does
not demonstrate that a person can obtain the predicted income, credit
status, or physiological change. Costs do not measure individualized money,
time, access, or burden, and the models do not capture dynamic adaptation
or uncertainty in treatment response.

The computational diabetes benchmark is particularly constrained: changing
a measurement in an SCM is not evidence of an effective clinical action.
No result in this paper supports treatment recommendations. Likewise,
omitting protected features from direct interventions does not prove
counterfactual fairness or absence of harmful downstream effects.

\subsection{Comparison breadth and uncertainty}
This study uses fixed saved models and a small deterministic subset of
their rejected records. Search-seed variation does not represent variation
over population samples or training splits. Incomplete Adult repetitions,
the diabetes exceptions, and the Adult DiCE anomaly prevent a clean claim
of uniform search stability. Unequal compute budgets and the lack of an
observational MINT comparison limit algorithmic rankings. The synthetic
audit adds a known mechanism but remains a stylized test rather than a
substitute for a domain-validated causal model.

The repository includes a user-study protocol and synthetic responses
generated to exercise an analysis pipeline. Those responses are not human
observations and are excluded from the empirical evidence. We report no
measured improvement in trust, comprehension, human outcomes, or actual
ability to execute a recommendation.

\section{Conclusion}
The saved experiments show that predictive validity, replay success,
actionability, and fidelity answer different questions. \CAC{} supplies
usable plans for every applicant in the canonical shared-model comparison,
but its ties with baselines, nonuniform seed behavior, and sensitivity to
fitted equations delimit that result. An intervention representation makes
causal assumptions explicit; it cannot make those assumptions true.

Evaluation should therefore retain declared actions, audit their replay,
state the usability conjunction, keep failed applicants in denominators,
and distinguish search variation from applicant-level inference. The
verified tables provide an auditable account of this implementation and
a concrete basis for a subsequent equal-budget, disjoint-model,
provenance-controlled study.
