ABSTRACT Flipping a machine learning model’s prediction does not mean an applicant received an
actionable plan. Real-world features are causally coupled; changing one variable shifts downstream attributes
and breaks naive recommendations upon execution. We audited algorithmic recourse over 294 rejected
individuals across four tabular benchmarks (HELOC, Adult, German, Diabetes) and two synthetic controls.
Rather than searching for coordinate shifts in unconstrained space, we evaluate actions within a structural
causal model. We test whether proposed interventions survive downstream causal propagation while re-
specting physical constraints like non-decreasing age, separating nominal proposal validity ( V ) from causal
replay validity ( R), physical actionability ( A), and manifold ﬁdelity ( F). Our central audit criterion is joint
usability (U = R∧ A), requiring candidate plans to simultaneously survive structural replay and feasibility
bounds. On credit risk (HELOC), Wachter gradient search achieves near-perfect nominal validity, yet joint
usability drops to just 18.0% because secondary credit features shift during replay. Searching directly over
valid action grids with CAC recovers 100% usability at default seed. Across 5 random seeds, optimizer
convergence failures reduce diabetes usability to 94.4% (with a single-run low of 72.1%), while reﬁtted
causal equations degrade usability to 93.0% on Adult and 97.0% on HELOC. Machine learning systems
should audit recourse proposals under causal propagation rather than relying on nominal validity alone.

tions, Explainable artiﬁcial intelligence, Structural causal models.
I. INTRODUCTION
W
HEN a machine learning classiﬁer denies an appli-
cation, algorithmic recourse calculates an actionable
set of feature changes to reverse the decision [ 1], [2]. Rather
than simply reporting static feature importances, the goal is
to provide an applicant with practical interventions that lead
to model approval [ 3]–[5].
Y et the vast majority of recourse formulations treat this
problem as a simple geometry exercise [ 4], [ 6]. Given a
rejected feature vector x and a ﬁxed black-box f , standard
algorithms minimize an L1 or L2 distance penalty to discover
a nearby point z satisfying f (z) = 1 . The core problem with
this approach is coordinate independence. In real-world data-
generating processes, features do not vary in a vacuum; they
remain tightly bound by physical, social, and institutional
mechanisms [ 7], [ 8]. A job applicant who acquires a post-
graduate degree does not hold their age, professional tenure,
or salary expectations frozen in time [ 9]. When an individual
carries out an action a, real-world rules propagate that change
downstream, producing an actual state ~x that rarely matches
the optimizer’s intended target z. If the classiﬁcation model
rejects this propagated state ~x—or if the downstream shifts
breach directional boundaries like non-decreasing age—the
entire recourse plan fails.

Existing recourse benchmarks hide this failure mode be-
hind aggregate reporting shortcuts. First, current toolkits
equate proposal validity (f (z) = 1 ) with true actionable
recourse (f (~x) = 1 ), crediting an algorithm with success even
when its advice fails under causal replay [ 10], [11]. Second,
evaluations routinely audit validity, directional feasibility, and
data-manifold distance as separate, independent rates across
an entire pool of generated candidates. An algorithm thus
receives full credit if candidate A clears the decision bound-
ary while an unrelated candidate B respects feasibility rules,
despite the fact that no single plan resolves the applicant’s
rejection. Third, reported benchmarks rarely test algorithmic
stability across random seeds or check sensitivity against
reﬁtted structural equations, masking optimizer crashes and
fragile dependency assumptions.
To address these gaps, this work presents a systematic
audit of Causal Actionable Counterfactuals (CAC) across
four standardized decision benchmarks—Adult Income, Ger-
man Credit, FICO HELOC, and Pima Diabetes—spanning
294 rejected individuals, alongside two synthetic benchmarks
with known causal graphs. Instead of perturbing arbitrary
coordinates in continuous feature space, CAC restricts its
search to admissible interventional sets a = ( S, vS) embed-
ded within an explicit Structural Causal Model ( M). Each
candidate action is evaluated directly on its topologically
propagated post-intervention state ~x =TM(x, a). We formal-
ize a decoupled audit suite that isolates proposal validity ( V ),
causal replay validity ( R), directional action feasibility ( A),
and coordinate ﬁdelity ( F), centering our primary evaluation
on joint usability (U = R∧ A), which strictly requires that
the same candidate recommendation simultaneously achieves
model approval and domain constraints.
Our contributions are centered on empirical evaluation and
interventional robustness:
1) Decoupled Audit Protocol: We formalize distinct ver-
iﬁcation criteria for nominal validity, causal replay,
directional feasibility, and coordinate drift, establishing
applicant-level joint usability ( U = R∧ A) as the
necessary benchmark metric.
2) Empirical Recourse Failure: Auditing 294 rejected
test cases reveals that while conventional methods
claim up to 100% nominal validity, true joint usability
drops to 18.0% on FICO HELOC under Wachter’s
method. In contrast, interventional action search via
CAC reaches 100% usability across all datasets at the
primary seed.
3) Multi-Seed Optimizer Auditing: Audits across 5 ran-
dom seeds uncover critical optimizer vulnerabilities in
diabetes, where numerical convergence failures reduce
mean usability to 94.4% (with a minimum of 72.1%).
4) Causal Sensitivity and Pipeline Hygiene: Reﬁtting
structural equations causes usability to slip to 93.0%
on Adult and 97.0% on HELOC, demonstrating that
model-based recourse guarantees remain bound to the
assumed equations. We further document data hygiene
hazards in standard pipelines, including pre-split impu-
tation and test-triggered hyperparameter selection.
The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows:
Section II contrasts point-based and causal recourse frame-
works; Section III details causal replay mechanics, the multi-
objective formulation, and evolutionary action search; Sec-
tion IV establishes our experimental testbed and statistical
protocol; Section V presents the empirical audits, multi-seed
stability evaluations, and ablation studies; Section VI ana-
lyzes the mechanisms driving recourse failure and examines
threats to validity; and Section VII concludes with method-
ological recommendations for the ﬁeld.
II. RELATED WORK
Algorithmic recourse literature divides along a clear line:
whether an explanation treats feature coordinates as indepen-
dent dials or respects the downstream mechanisms linking
them. Table 1 compares common paradigms across search
entities, replay propagation, constraint structures, and audit
protocols.
A. POINT-BASED COUNTERFACTUALS AND
ACTIONABILITY CONSTRAINTS
The foundational formulation by Wachter et al. [4] casts
recourse as unconstrained gradient optimization in feature
space, minimizing a continuous distance penalty to ﬂip a
prediction. Subsequent works introduced diversity mecha-
nisms via determinantal point processes (DiCE [6]), enforced
linear feasibility rules via integer programming (Ustun et
al. [3]), routed recommendations along empirical data graphs
(FACE [12]), or guided search vectors using class prototypes
(V an Looveren and Klaise [13]).
Y et all coordinate-based strategies share a core limitation:
they perturb feature coordinates in isolation, assuming un-
affected attributes remain ﬁxed at their original values. In
reality, intervening on an actionable feature triggers down-
stream changes across related variables. Because point-based
algorithms optimize directly over input targets z rather than
executable interventional operators a, their suggested states
frequently occupy impossible regions of the data distribution
or fail once real-world dynamics take place.
B. CAUSAL ALGORITHMIC RECOURSE AND
INTERVENTIONS
Pearl’s interventional calculus [9] establishes the formal ma-
chinery required to model post-action consequences. Build-
ing on this framework, Karimi et al. [7] proved that viable
recourse demands interventional operators do(XS = vS)
rather than conditional observations, subsequently extending
this formulation to handle probabilistic uncertainty in the un-
derlying causal equations (MINT [ 8]). Related foundational
analyses by Barocas et al. [5] and Karimi et al. [1] examined
the implicit assumptions governing causal and normative re-
course.
Despite these theoretical advances, current empirical eval-
uations have serious auditing gaps. Studies regularly compute
validity, actionability, and manifold distance as independent

C. BENCHMARKING SUITES, TOOLKITS, AND
ROBUSTNESS
The development of standardized benchmarking libraries
such as CARLA [ 10] and Alibi Explain [ 14] brought much-
needed modularity to counterfactual research. In parallel,
multi-objective evolutionary formulations like NSGA-II [15],
[16] emerged to balance sparsity against proximity, while
Upadhyay et al. [11] examined recourse vulnerability under
model shifts.
Nonetheless, current benchmarking frameworks evaluate
algorithms almost exclusively on single random seeds, omit
causal descendant veriﬁcation, and ignore optimizer conver-
gence exceptions. Our study addresses these methodological
blind spots: we implement paired applicant-level statistical
tests, record query costs and numerical exceptions explicitly,
and track whether recommendations withstand causal replay
under reﬁtted data-generating equations.
III. PROPOSED METHOD AND SYSTEM DESIGN
We now formalize the interventional recourse framework,
downstream structural replay mechanisms, decoupled audit
metrics, and the genetic action search implemented in CAC.
A. RECOURSE FORMULATION AND SYSTEM
ARCHITECTURE
Let x = ( x1, . . . ,xd ) ∈ X represent an individual’s ob-
served proﬁle across a mixed-type input space. A black-
box classiﬁer f : X → {0, 1} predicts class probabilities
p+(x) = P(Y = 1 | X = x), assigning an approval
f (x) = 1{p+(x) ≥ τ} at threshold τ = 0 .5. Our audit
focuses exclusively on applicants who receive an initial re-
jection, f (x) = 0 .
A recourse recommendation pairs a target feature vector
with an explicit intervention: (z, a), where a = ( S, vS). The
active index set S ⊆ {1, . . . ,d} isolates variables directly
manipulated by the applicant, while vS = {vj}j∈S assigns
their replacement values. Fig. 1 details this operational ﬂow.
Instead of treating recourse as an unconstrained drift across
continuous space, CAC restricts optimization strictly to ad-
missible actions a = ( S, vS), preventing the search from
proposing coordinate shifts that implicitly hold causally de-
pendent descendants ﬁxed.
B. STRUCTURAL CAUSAL MODELS AND DESCENDANT
REPLA Y
A Structural Causal Model M = ( S, PU ) governs data
generation over observed variables X ={X1, . . . ,Xd} and
exogenous background noise U ={U1, . . . ,Ud}∼ PU . A
Directed Acyclic Graph deﬁnes functional relationships:
Xj = fj(PAj, Uj), ∀j∈{ 1, . . . ,d}, (1)
where PAj⊆ X\{ Xj} denotes the direct causal parents of
Xj. To determine how an action alters an applicant’s proﬁle in
reality, we simulate Pearl’s counterfactual engine [ 9] across
three sequential phases:
1) Abduction: Recover the unobserved background fac-
tors ux = ( u1, . . . ,ud ) characterizing applicant x. For
continuous features, we invert ﬁtted equations to com-

preserve conditional probability quantiles.
2) Action: Apply the intervention do(XS = vS), which
overwrites structural equations for intervened features

E. EVOLUTIONARY SEARCH AND ARCHIVE SELECTION
Because gradient-boosted tree ensembles partition fea-
ture space into non-differentiable step plateaus, traditional
gradient-based optimizers quickly stall in local optima. CAC
bypasses this limitation using an elitist genetic algorithm
deﬁned directly over discrete quantile grids. The population
maintains 60 candidate interventions initialized with single-
feature actions and sparse combinations. At each generation,
the ﬁttest 50% elite actions are preserved directly. Offspring
generated via uniform crossover and mutation are snapped
onto admissible quantile grids G and restricted to at most 3
simultaneous interventions.
The search terminates when reaching 200 generations or
when progress stalls across 20 consecutive generations. V alid,
feasible plans are accumulated in external Pareto archive A.
If fewer than k candidates achieve validity, the lowest-loss
rejected candidates are retained as diagnostics, though the
audit strictly tallies them as failures. Algorithm 1 formalizes
the routine, and Fig. 3 sketches the execution loop. Discrete
quantile search explores non-convex combinatorial spaces
reliably without computing unstable numerical gradients over
tree splits.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
To evaluate interventional recourse across distinct data
regimes, we benchmark four tabular tasks representing di-
verse dimensionalities, causal graph densities, and institu-
tional constraints.

4) Pima Diabetes [ 21]: An 8-feature clinical diag-

D. BASELINE RECOURSE ALGORITHMS
We benchmark CAC against four established recourse al-
gorithms under strictly identical immutability masks, action
budgets, and directional bounds:
1) Random Search: Uniformly samples action subsets
from legal quantile lattices, returning the minimum-
effort candidate satisfying model acceptance.
2) Wachter-Style Optimization: Executes multi-start L-
BFGS-B in continuous feature space, estimating model
gradients via numerical ﬁnite differences.
3) DiCE-Random [ 6]: Generates sets of diverse coun-
terfactual perturbations using determinantal point pro-
cesses.
4) Grid Search (‘‘Oracle’’ Reference):Exhaustively tra-
verses allowable quantile changes ordered by increas-
ing normalized effort until exhausting a preset query
budget.
5) MINT [8]: Deployed in synthetic control environments
where true structural equations are analytically speci-
ﬁed, matching its interventional assumptions.
Because the Grid Search baseline operates under a capped
query ceiling, its performance reﬂects bounded exploration
rather than guaranteed global optimality. In parallel, our com-
parisons reﬂect speciﬁc reference implementations; alterna-
tive optimizers or penalty schedules could yield different
trade-offs.
E. STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE PROTOCOL
At default seed 42, we evaluate paired binary applicant us-
ability outcomes ( Uim) via exact two-sided McNemar tests,
controlling the familywise error rate within each domain
using Holm step-down adjustments ( α = 0 .05). To quantify
multi-seed variability across shared runs without parametric
assumptions, we calculate applicant-level paired difference
distributions using 10,000 bootstrap resamples at bootstrap
seed 42.

E. COMPONENT ABLATION ANAL YSIS
Table 6 isolates the functional contributions of our multi-
objective terms at seed 42. Disabling interventional updates
(scoring candidates directly on naive coordinate shifts) de-
grades usability to 95.0% on Adult and 96.0% on HELOC,

indispensable when mutable features have downstream credit
descendants, whereas loosely coupled graphs permit naive
shifts without severe penalty.
Removing the action effort term ( λc = 0 ) increases

interventions. In contrast, zeroing the soft feasibility penalty

coordinate projection during grid legalization fully guaran-

usability stays higher simply because an individual needs only
one surviving plan among their top k candidates. Because
the reﬁtted equations were trained on overlapping data splits,
this drop represents a conservative lower bound on real-world
sensitivity.

bounds. MINT attains lower intervention effort in both en-

minimal-effort recourse.

G. RESUL T CHECKS AND VERIFICATION PROTOCOL
To ensure reproducibility and safeguard against artifact cor-
ruption, all ﬁndings were audited against four operational
checks:
1) Fixed Query Alignment: Every method is evaluated
on the identical set of 294 rejected test individuals
across all benchmarks, eliminating sample selection
bias.
2) Causal State Replay: All candidate interventions are
passed through non-linear SCM equations TM(x, a)
to simulate true downstream proﬁle updates prior to
classiﬁer scoring.
3) Coupled Joint Usability: The audit marks an applicant
as served if and only if the identical plan satisﬁes both
classiﬁer acceptance ( R = 1 ) and feasibility bounds
(A = 1).
4) Software Test Coverage: The execution pipeline
cleared 386 automated unit and property tests with 1
documented skip, guaranteeing algorithmic and statis-
tical consistency across all generated assets.
Section VI analyzes the mechanistic roots of these em-
pirical outcomes and details limitations of the benchmark
pipeline.

VI. DISCUSSION
The empirical results indicate that recourse viability depends
critically on how strongly features interact within the under-
lying domain.
A. MECHANISTIC INTERPRETATION OF EMPIRICAL
FINDINGS
Our empirical ﬁndings demonstrate why nominal validity (V )
is a fundamentally ﬂawed surrogate for actionable recourse.
On HELOC, Wachter’s method achieves 85.0% proposal va-
lidity, yet true joint usability drops to 18.0%. The mechan-
ics driving this breakdown are clear: point-based optimizers
exploit classiﬁer boundaries by treating features as isolated
coordinates. In credit underwriting, however, an applicant
cannot simply inﬂate their repayment history without trig-
gering corresponding changes in revolving credit utilization
and bureau inquiries. When these interventional side effects
propagate through the structural equations, the replayed state
~x either falls back across the rejection boundary or breaches
directional rules. This divergence explains the gap between
V and U , proving that unconstrained mathematical perturba-
tions cannot be assumed to reﬂect physical actions.
Conversely, non-causal algorithms perform surprisingly
well on Adult Income and German Credit. Structural inspec-
tion of the underlying graphs (Table 9) reveals why: both

B. METHODOLOGICAL AND DATA PREPROCESSING
LIMITATIONS
A rigorous code-level audit revealed three data-hygiene prac-
tices common across recourse research that require scrutiny:
1) Test-Gated Hyperparameter Decisions: In conven-
tional pipelines, test-set AUC was monitored to deter-
mine whether to trigger hyperparameter tuning. While
inner cross-validation folds were utilized during op-
timization, allowing test-split performance to dictate
whether search is performed introduces subtle data
leakage across splits.
2) Pre-Split Feature Imputation: Missing values across
HELOC and Diabetes were imputed using global col-
umn medians computed prior to train-test partition-
ing. Beyond this, diabetes cleaning leveraged class-
conditional patterns to ﬂag sentinel missingness, poten-
tially allowing label information to leak across evalua-
tion boundaries.
3) Physiological Validity of Diabetes Recourse: In-
verting classiﬁcation targets (no_diabetes=1-Outcome)
and treating diagnostic clinical measurements (glucose,
insulin, blood pressure, BMI) as directly actionable di-
als contradicts medical reality. Biomarkers are clinical
signs of underlying metabolic processes, not voluntary
personal decisions [21]. We retain Pima Diabetes solely
to reproduce benchmark comparisons established in
prior recourse literature [ 10], [ 14], emphasizing that
these feature changes must never be interpreted as ther-
apeutic interventions.
C. THREATS TO VALIDITY
We identify three methodological boundaries that frame these
ﬁndings:
1) Construct Validity: Our effort metric C(x, a) com-
putes normalized coordinate distances, serving as a
mathematical proxy rather than capturing ﬁnancial fric-
tion, lost time, or emotional strain. Synthetic scripts
simulating interactive user preference responses were
excluded from our empirical claims.
2) Internal Validity: Numerical gradient estimation via
ﬁnite differences is poorly conditioned on the discon-
tinuous piecewise-constant loss landscapes of tree en-
sembles, putting Wachter-style optimization at a severe
numerical disadvantage. In addition, the reﬁt exper-
iment (Table 7) shared training observations across
models, meaning observed usability drops represent
lower-bound estimates of real-world degradation.
3) External Validity: Assumed causal DAGs represent
domain hypotheses rather than experimentally vali-
dated mechanisms. Because several edges lack obser-
vational orientation support (Table 9), a plan that suc-
ceeds underTM may still fail if the underlying struc-
tural equations are misspeciﬁed.
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
Across 294 rejected test cases spanning four tabular bench-
marks and two synthetic domains, our evaluation demon-
strates that nominal counterfactual validity is an unreliable
proxy for actionable recourse. On FICO HELOC, point-
based methods claim near-perfect validity while joint us-
ability drops to 18.0%. Searching directly over admissible
actions within a structural causal model, CAC achieves 100%
usability across all benchmarks at the primary seed.
Y et multi-seed audits and sensitivity checks reveal clear
limits. Convergence exceptions drop minimum diabetes us-
ability to 72.1%, while reﬁtting structural equations degrades
usability to 93.0% on Adult and 97.0% on HELOC. Model-
based recourse remains bound to the accuracy of the underly-
ing causal mechanisms.
Future research must target three priorities: benchmark
suites should mandate decoupled audits ( V , R, A, F, U ) with
joint plan satisfaction, pipelines must partition data prior to
imputation or parameter selection, and recourse algorithms
must be designed to withstand uncertain or misspeciﬁed
causal graphs.
